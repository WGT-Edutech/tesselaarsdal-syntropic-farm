The Problem

Black Wattle Is Stealing Our Water

South Africa is the 31st driest country in the world. Invasive alien plants — primarily black wattle (Acacia mearnsii) — cover vast swathes of Western Cape catchment areas, consuming massive volumes of water that would otherwise feed dams and aquifers.

The Scale

Black wattle is one of the most widespread invasive alien trees in South Africa. Clearing mature infestations increases available water resources by 15–29.5%.

The Cost

Invasive species have overrun priority water catchment regions. Their removal is critical to improve dam inflows for Cape Town's 4.8 million residents.

At the same time, conventional agriculture degrades soil, depends on expensive inputs, and is increasingly vulnerable to climate shocks. Small-scale farmers lack access to proven regenerative methods and the capital to transition.

The Solution

Syntropic Farming: Agriculture That Heals

Syntropic farming, developed by Ernst Götsch, is the most intensive form of agroforestry. It choreographs plant succession through time — mimicking how a forest naturally regenerates — to produce food while restoring soil, water cycles, and biodiversity.

Wattle Clearing

Remove invasive black wattle from the property and surrounding catchment. Revenue from the FirstRand/RMB bond funds the clearing and provides long-term ecosystem service income.

Syntropic Systems

Establish stratified agroforestry rows combining fruit trees, timber, vegetables, and support species. The 1,600 m² terraced area expands into a full syntropic food forest.

Aquaponics

Build landscaped aquaponics ponds combining fish, plants, and water features. Symbiotic polyculture with poultry and small livestock integrated into the system.

Climate-Controlled Areas

Greenhouses and shade houses for rare plant propagation, seedling nurseries, and season extension. Enables year-round production and biodiversity conservation.

Biodynamic 100%

Full biodynamic certification pathway. Closed-loop fertility: compost, worm farms, biochar, and on-farm inputs only. Zero external fertilisers or pesticides.

Education & Training

Teach people how to grow, harvest, and secure their future. Workshops, internships, and a training curriculum for aspiring syntropic farmers.

Verification & Monitoring

Proving Water Retention & Soil Moisture

Investors, bond partners, and carbon markets require verifiable proof that the farm is retaining soil moisture and using less water. The project uses a multi-layered monitoring approach — from ground sensors to satellite remote sensing.

Soil Moisture Monitoring

In-Ground Sensors

  • Capacitance probes at 10cm, 30cm, 60cm depths — continuous moisture readings
  • Tensiometers — measure soil water tension (how hard plants must work to extract water)
  • TDR/FDR sensors — time-domain reflectometry for precise volumetric water content
  • Data loggers with GSM/LoRa uplink — real-time dashboard accessible to investors and researchers

Soil Health Indicators

  • Infiltration rate tests — double-ring infiltrometer: how fast water enters the soil
  • Soil organic carbon (SOC) — annual lab tests; higher SOC = higher water holding capacity
  • Bulk density — compacted soil holds less water; tracked annually
  • Earthworm counts — simple, visual indicator of soil biology and structure
  • Penetrometer readings — measures soil compaction

Water Balance Tracking

Inputs

  • Borehole flow meter — total water pumped (L/day)
  • Rainfall gauge — on-site weather station
  • Irrigation metering — per-zone water use

Outputs & Losses

  • Dam level sensors — monitor water storage
  • Evapotranspiration (ET) estimates — calculated from weather data
  • Runoff monitoring — compare cleared vs. vegetated areas
  • Soil moisture trend — the key metric: is moisture increasing over time?

Remote Sensing & Satellite Verification

  • NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) — satellite-based vegetation health; shows greening over time as syntropic systems mature
  • Land Surface Temperature (LST) — cooler soil temperatures indicate higher moisture content and transpiration
  • SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar) — penetrates cloud cover; directly measures soil moisture from space
  • Sentinel-2 / Landsat — free satellite imagery for annual land cover classification (wattle clearing progress, reforestation)
  • Evapotranspiration mapping — verify actual water use vs. conventional agriculture

MRV (Monitoring, Reporting, Verification)

Carbon credit MRV: For carbon credit revenue, a formal MRV pipeline is required. This combines field sensor data, soil sampling, and satellite imagery to produce verified carbon sequestration numbers. Programs like Orizon CarbonCrop and Cultivating Carbon handle this for South African farms — they provide the platform, verification, and credit issuance. The farm's sensor data feeds directly into this pipeline.

Land Stewardship Trust

A Trust of Land Stewards Pitching Projects for Approval

The project is structured as a Land Stewardship Trust — a legal entity that oversees multiple properties and projects across the Western Cape. Land stewards (farmers, land managers, community groups) pitch projects to the trust for approval, funding, and support. The trust ensures alignment with the mission: revitalisation and nurturing of the land.

Why a Trust Structure?

Alignment with South African Law

South Africa's Biodiversity Stewardship framework (SANBI / CapeNature) offers 5 agreement types — from informal partnership areas to fully declared Nature Reserves. The trust can serve as the coordinating body for multiple stewardship agreements, giving land stewards legal recognition and access to incentives.

Scalable Model

Rather than one farm, the trust becomes a portfolio of regenerative projects. Each land steward manages their property; the trust provides verification, funding access, carbon credit aggregation, and the RMB bond relationship. Impact multiplies with each approved project.

Trust Structure

Legal Entity

A Non-Profit Company (NPC) or Trust under South African law, registered with CIPC. The trust holds the master agreements with the RMB bond / TNC SA, carbon programs, and Biodiversity Stewardship authorities. Individual land stewards operate under sub-agreements with the trust.

How It Works

1. Application
Land stewards submit a project proposal: property details, current state, proposed interventions (wattle clearing, syntropic, aquaponics, etc.), and expected outcomes. Applications are accepted on a rolling basis.
2. Assessment
The trust evaluates each proposal against criteria: biodiversity value, water catchment priority, soil degradation level, social impact, and feasibility. A site visit and baseline assessment are conducted.
3. Approval & Agreement
Approved projects receive a stewardship agreement. The trust assigns a Biodiversity Stewardship level (Conservation Area, Biodiversity Agreement, or higher). The land steward commits to a management plan with measurable targets.
4. Support & Funding
The trust channels resources: RMB bond clearing funds, carbon credit pre-payments, grant funding, technical assistance, and training. The trust aggregates carbon credits across all properties for better pricing.
5. Verification & Reporting
The trust conducts annual audits and monitoring (the MRV system described above). Results are reported to funders, bond partners, and stewards. High-performing stewards receive additional support.
